Travel · Moving

Moving to a new apartment with a cat: relocate, do not replace — the familiar stations move as they are, and the new place opens one room at a time.

Your cat is not new to you; only the place is new to the cat. Set up one starter room completely, move the bed, lounge, and pan exactly as they stand, and keep the old routine running while the rooms around it change.

The honest answer: the cat is not new, only the place is

This guide is the other half of a case our new-cat starter kit does not cover. The starter kit is for a cat new to you: every station bought new, placed in arrival order, learned from nothing. A move is the reverse. The cat already knows its pan, lounge, and bed — by smell, by position, by routine. What it does not know is the apartment they now stand in — and replacing the gear throws away its one advantage.

So the honest core of a move is relocate, do not replace. The stations travel as they are, because their familiarity is the whole value they carry on moving day: a new bed in a new room is two changes at once; the old bed in a new room is one. Everything here follows from that — one room made familiar first, the old arrangement rebuilt inside it, and the old clock kept running underneath the boxes.

None of that requires a purchase. This guide names only the three anchor stations, in the form the site already names them: if yours are the same models, keep them and skip the links. If one will not survive the move or fit the new room, the picks below are its like-for-like replacements.

Check one: one room first

Choose the starter room before moving day. In most small apartments it is the bedroom, or the quietest room with a door that closes. Finishing matters more than size: a small room with every station in place beats a large room with half the stations still in boxes, because the cat reads completeness on arrival.

Set that room up completely before the cat arrives. Litter in its corner, food and water apart from it, a scratcher, a bed in the quiet corner. Then the cat starts there, and only there. The rest opens room by room, at the cat’s pace: a door opened when the starter room is being used confidently — eating, using the pan, sleeping in the open — and closed again without drama if the cat retreats. Retreat is information.

The one-room start also contains the learning: a missed pan and scratching on new furniture happen in the room you prepared, instead of across every room at once. By the time the second room opens, the first is a known base — and a cat with a base explores; a cat without one hides.

Check two: familiar stations, moved as-is

Move the stations as they are. The bed and the lounge travel unwashed: their smell is the cat’s own scent, laid down over months, and in a new room it is the strongest signal that this place is also theirs. Washing belongs later, once the cat has settled; our washing pet beds guide covers that wash. Before the move, a wash deletes the asset you most need on arrival.

Then rebuild the old map inside the new walls: the pan in a corner, the lounge parallel to the sofa, the bed in the quiet corner, in the same relative arrangement as the old place. The walls, light, and sounds are all new — but the relationships between the stations can be right, and a cat navigating by that map finds each station where it has always been. Familiar geometry makes a new apartment legible on the first night instead of the tenth.

The like-for-like rule has one honest exception: if a station will not survive the move — a pan cracked in transit, a lounge shredded through — or does not fit the new room, replace that one station with the nearest equivalent, measuring first. One replaced station among familiar ones is a small change; a whole new set is the move’s most common unforced error. The pair below anchor the starter room: the pan, and the lounge the cat can scratch from the first hour.

Check three: litter first, everything else in its old routine

Of everything in the starter room, the pan is set up first — before the bed, before the lounge, before the cat is anywhere near the building if you can manage it. It is the one station needed without delay on arrival, and it goes in its permanent corner, not a temporary spot: a pan that moves a week later teaches the lesson twice, in a home the cat was just beginning to trust. Our litter box placement guide works the corner choice in depth; the moving rule is to make it once and keep it.

Around the pan, keep every separation the cat already knows. Food and water stand apart from the litter, as they did in the old place — cats do not eat beside the toilet by choice, and a move is the wrong week to test whether yours will. The apartment litter and travel guide makes the same point for weekends away: the familiar setup beats a new arrangement introduced at the moment of change.

Finally, keep the clock. Feeding and play times stay on the old schedule where you can hold them: the routine is the one large piece of the old life in your control, and a cat whose day still runs on time has a frame to hang the new rooms on. Expect quieter first days, and let the schedule be an offer the cat rejoins gradually. The bed below is the last station: quiet corner, carried over unwashed, left for the cat to choose.

The moving-day sequence

Set up the starter room first. Before the cat travels, the starter room is finished: pan first in its permanent corner, food and water apart from it, lounge in the old arrangement, bed unwashed in the quiet corner. This room stays shut and quiet while the rest of the move happens.

The cat travels last. The cat is the final trip, not the first: it leaves after the noise at the old apartment is done and arrives to a finished room, not a work site. Go straight to the starter room on arrival.

Door closed, and the cat chooses. Put the carrier down in the starter room, open it, close the door, and leave the timing to the cat. Some cats walk out and inspect the stations in order; some stay in the carrier for hours. Both are normal first evenings. Keep visitors out, and let emerging, eating, and the first use of the pan happen in whatever order the cat picks.

Open one more room at a time. From the next day onward, expansion is gradual: one more room opened when the starter room is being used confidently, its door left open behind the cat as the way back. Keep the stations where they were placed — the map stays fixed while the territory grows.

Buy or skip

A move is the rare guide here where the default purchase is nothing: the gear that settles a cat is the gear already standing in the old apartment. The panels below are for the exceptions.

Buy only if

  • A station genuinely will not survive the move. A cracked pan or a lounge shredded through earns a like-for-like replacement — same job, same corner, nearest equivalent form.
  • A station does not fit the new room. Measure the new corner and doorways first; if the old station honestly cannot fit, replace that one station on fit, not on looks.
  • The old home never had an anchor station. If there was never a proper pan, scratcher, or bed, the move is a fair moment to add the missing one, in the starter room from day one.

Skip if

  • The plan is a whole new gear set for the new place. New gear on a new place is two changes at once; the familiar set settles the cat faster than any matching new one.
  • The old stations are merely worn. Wear is familiarity here: the lounge scratched for a year carries more settling value than its pristine replacement. Replace on failure or fit, not appearance.
  • You are buying to solve first-days behaviour. Hiding and cautious exploring in the first days are a move working normally, not a missing product. Give the routine time first.

When it is not a gear problem

A move unsettles cats, and the first days are allowed to look like it: hiding, eating less, exploring at night. That arc should bend, though — a little more of the old cat each day. When it does not bend, the boundary is the starter kit’s own. A cat that is not eating, not using the pan, or hiding without let-up past the first days needs a veterinarian, not more gear.

FAQ

Should I buy new gear when I move apartments with my cat?

No. A new place is already one large change, and new gear on top of it is a second change landing on the same day. Move the stations the cat already uses, exactly as they are, and buy only a like-for-like replacement if one will not survive the move or does not fit the new room. Measure first, so the replacement is chosen on fit, not on moving-week panic.

Why should my cat start in only one room of the new apartment?

Because one finished room is a map the cat can learn in an evening, and a whole new apartment is not. Set the starter room up completely — litter, food and water apart, a scratcher, a bed — and let the cat settle there first. Then open the rest room by room, as the cat chooses. A cat that owns one room has a base to return to; a cat released into every room at once has nowhere settled yet.

Should I wash my cat’s bed and lounge before the move?

No. Travel them unwashed. On moving day the familiar smell on the bed and the lounge is the point — it is the part of the old home the cat can still find in the new one. Washing belongs after the cat has settled. The washing guide covers that later wash; before the move, leave the stations exactly as the cat last used them.

Where should the litter pan go in the new apartment?

In its permanent corner, set up first, before the cat arrives if you can. Choose the corner the pan will keep — not a temporary spot it will move from in a week — because a moved pan restarts the lesson in a home that is already new. Keep food and water apart from it, and prop the flap open at first if your cat needs that.

What if my cat will not eat or use the litter pan after the move?

Give the first days some patience: a quiet starter room and the old feeding and play times. But a cat that is not eating, not using the pan, or hiding without let-up past the first days is not a gear problem, and more equipment will not solve it. That cat needs a veterinarian, not a purchase.
