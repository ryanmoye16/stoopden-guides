Play · Kittens

Teaching a kitten where to scratch in a small apartment: give the wake-up scratch an address beside the bed, start with both axes, and redirect — never punish.

A kitten will scratch — to shed claw sheaths, to stretch, and to leave its mark where it lives. The lesson is never whether to scratch, only where. Put the first scratcher beside the sleep spot, offer a horizontal and a vertical surface while you learn which axis the kitten chooses, and make the right address easier and more rewarding than the sofa corner it will otherwise graduate to.

What changes with a kitten

Our play guides were built for the general case: read the damage on the real sofa, match that motion, and put the sacrificial object on the stolen corner. Our scratcher sofa vs posts guide runs that reading in full, and our stop-scratching guide is the repair routine. A kitten changes the job: there is no crime scene yet. The habit is still forming, so you choose the first address instead of competing with an established one.

Start from what scratching is. A kitten scratches to shed the worn outer sheath of a growing claw, to stretch its shoulders, back and legs after sleep, and to mark — scent glands in the paws and a visible score both say this place is lived in. None of that is misbehaviour, and none of it can be trained away. The only question your apartment answers is which surface receives it.

A small apartment helps more than it hurts. A kitten’s first world is the starter room our new-cat starter kit sets up — sleep, food, water, litter and play within a few steps — so one or two surfaces cover its whole territory. The risk is the same closeness: the sofa corner is also a few steps away, and a kitten does not distinguish legal from illegal furniture. It distinguishes near from far. Set the legal surfaces nearer than the alternatives, on the routes the kitten already walks.

The wake-up scratch

The most predictable scratch of a kitten’s day happens in its first waking minute. Kittens scratch right after waking — wake, stretch, scratch, in that order, at the end of every nap. Our kitten sleep guide is built around those deep bursts between play and meals; each burst ends with the same raking stretch, aimed at whatever stands within a few steps of where the kitten slept. Repetition turns nearest into habitual within days.

So the first scratcher goes beside the sleep spot, not beside the sofa damage. That reverses the adult logic on purpose: an adult’s scratcher belongs on the stolen corner because that is where the habit lives, but a kitten’s habit does not live anywhere yet, and its most frequent scratch starts at the bed. In the starter room, the horizontal lounge stands right beside the bed, close enough that a waking kitten can step off the bed and rake it without crossing the room. The sofa corner gets its surface too — as the second address.

Use the rhythm instead of fighting it. When the kitten wakes and reaches for the bed edge or the rug, that reach is the lesson arriving on schedule: carry the kitten the few steps to the lounge and let its paws finish the scratch there. Do it at the morning wake-up and after the long afternoon sleep, and the address sets quickly — you are moving an inevitable behaviour a short distance, at the moment it was already happening. Our kitten play guide runs the same waking energy into play — the scratch comes first.

Start with both axes, small

An adult cat tells you its axis in upholstery: a forward rake along a cushion, or a stand-and-pull up an arm. A kitten has not declared one yet, and guessing wrong is the expensive mistake — a surface in the axis the kitten does not use gets ignored while the kitten improvises on furniture in the axis it does. Start with both axes, small: one horizontal cardboard surface the kitten can lie on and rake, and one vertical post it can stand against and pull down.

Watch that information as it arrives: which surface the kitten uses after waking, which after play, and which it walks past. Most kittens settle on one dominant axis within the first weeks; some genuinely use both, the way our play corner guide pairs them for adults. Let the use decide the bigger buy later, rather than furnishing both axes at full size on day one. The picks below are framed for that start: the lounge as the horizontal, first scratch address beside the bed, and the post as the vertical, full-stretch one. Be honest about the post’s size: a kitten grows into the 32 in post, working its lower section at first. The full stretch is the adult’s — the point of buying that height once, now, instead of replacing a short kitten post.

Reading the pair’s results takes a week, not a day. Cardboard crumbs beside the lounge mean the horizontal address is landing; pulled fibres and a kitten standing tall against the post mean the vertical one is. A surface that stays pristine while the other wears is the answer you were watching for — it stays as the spare while the winning axis earns the upgrade. Placement comes first, though: the next section is the test both surfaces have to pass.

Placement in a small apartment

Two addresses cover a kitten in a small apartment. The first is beside the sleep spot, for the wake-up scratch above. The second is beside the sofa corner the kitten will graduate to — the corner nearest its growing territory, on the route from the starter room into the living room. As the kitten’s world widens, that corner is where an unaddressed scratch will move. A surface already standing there, carrying the kitten’s scent and claw marks, is a claim made legally before the fabric was an option. Our scratcher placement guide works the adult version of these checks in full.

Stability is the check kittens punish fastest. The lounge must sit flat, with no rock or slide when the kitten rakes and lands on it, and the post must stand on a base that does not walk across hard floors when the kitten pulls. Test both with your own hard pull first. A post that tips or skates teaches that the real sofa is more stable than the object you offered — the opposite lesson — and a kitten a post has wobbled under will route around it for weeks. On hard floors, set the base against a wall or the sofa end so the room braces it.

The failing spots are predictable. A scratcher exiled to a spare corner the kitten never visits is storage, not a station. A lounge left across the kitchen-to-door walkway gets nudged aside and moved — and a moved scratcher loses the fixed address the kitten was learning. And no scratcher earns its place beside the litter pan: scratching belongs near sleep and along patrol routes, not beside the toilet. Defend that one separation — sleep and scratch at one end, litter at the other.

Redirect, never punish

The routine has three moves, run the same way by every person in the apartment. First, never punish: no scolding, no spray bottle. Punishment cannot teach a place, because the kitten connects it to you and your presence, not to the surface — the scratch still happens, just when you are at work. A kitten raking the sofa arm is not defying you — it is doing maintenance at the nearest address.

Second, redirect in the moment. Catch the scratch as it starts — the stretch toward the wrong surface, the paw lifted, the first rake — carry the kitten calmly to the right surface, and let its paws finish there. Reward the use the instant it happens on the legal surface: praise, a stroke, a moment of play, delivered while its claws are in the cardboard or the sisal, so the reward lands on the address. Repeat without irritation, and have the whole household run the same moves: a redirect that happens nine times out of ten teaches that the tenth is permitted.

Third, tilt the room. Make the wrong surface boring and the right one interesting: a temporary throw or cover over the targeted sofa corner removes the texture the kitten was enjoying while the lesson sets. It comes off once the legal address carries the habit. The right surface stands directly beside that covered corner or beside the bed, catching the wake-up scratch by geography, with every reward landing on it. Our stop-scratching guide runs this tilt as a repair; with a kitten you run it as prevention.

Claw care while the lesson sets

While the address is being learned, the claws need a parallel routine, because kitten claws are needle-sharp. They hook into sofa weave, clothing and carpet loops, they catch and panic a small kitten, and they do more damage per scratch than an adult’s — so the learning weeks are also the weeks of maximum fabric risk. Scratching sheds the worn sheath, but it does not blunt the tip. Trimming does: tiny amounts, often — just the sharp point, in short calm moments, even one or two claws at a time while the kitten is sleepy.

Fold the trimming into handling the kitten already accepts. Our kitten grooming guide builds that habit in tiny touches inside petting — paws held briefly, toes pressed gently so a claw extends, everything ended before any protest — and claw care belongs in the same sessions. Stop at the first squirm and finish tomorrow. If you have never trimmed claws, have a veterinarian or groomer show you the angle and the safe amount once before you start.

Keep the two routines in their lanes. Trimming is damage control while learning; the scratcher is the learning. A kitten with trimmed tips still needs to scratch every day — to shed sheaths, to stretch, to mark — and still needs the legal address beside its bed. Trimming buys a sofa that survives the teaching period. It never buys permission to skip the scratcher.

Buy or skip

An address taught early: the first scratcher beside the sleep spot, both axes started small, and every wrong scratch redirected — never punished.

Buy only if

  • The first scratcher can stand beside the sleep spot. The wake-up scratch lands within a few steps of the bed.
  • You will start with both axes. A horizontal surface and a vertical post, modest to begin with, while you watch which the kitten uses.
  • Every person will redirect the same way. Carry the kitten to the right surface mid-scratch, reward the use, never punish.
  • The base passes your own pull test. A post that does not walk on hard floors and a lounge that sits flat.

Skip if

  • You wanted to stop the scratching itself. Scratching is maintenance, stretching and marking. It can only be addressed, not ended.
  • The only free floor is a spare corner or a walkway. A scratcher the kitten never passes teaches no address.
  • You are buying one axis at full size on a guess. Start small on both, read a week of use, and let the scratch choose the upgrade.
  • The kitten’s scratching has suddenly changed. A break in the pattern is a vet visit first — see below.

When it is not a gear problem

A healthy kitten scratches loudly and often, as part of the same loud pattern as its play and appetite. The signal is a break in that pattern. A kitten that suddenly stops scratching and playing, goes off its food, chews one paw raw, limps, or cries when a paw is touched is not reporting the wrong scratcher — a torn claw, a sore pad and plain illness all sit behind changes like those, and kittens can go downhill quickly. That earns a veterinarian promptly.

The same applies to a claw caught and torn on fabric: a snagged kitten that panics can injure the claw it was caught by. Free it calmly if you safely can, and let a veterinarian check a bleeding, bent or dangling claw rather than trimming it yourself. Shop for the surfaces a well kitten learns on; let a vet handle sore paws and sudden quiet.

FAQ

Can you stop a kitten from scratching?

No, and you should not try. Scratching is maintenance, not misbehaviour: a kitten scratches to shed worn claw sheaths, to stretch its shoulders and back after sleep, and to leave both a visible mark and scent where it lives. The trainable part is the address. Give the scratch a legal surface in the right place from the first week and the kitten learns that surface — try to end the scratching itself and the kitten simply learns to scratch when you are not there.

Where should I put a kitten's first scratcher?

Beside the sleep spot, within a few steps of where the kitten wakes. Kittens scratch right after waking — the wake-up scratch is the most predictable scratch of the day — so the first surface the kitten meets on waking is the one that becomes the habit. Put a second surface beside the sofa corner the kitten will graduate to as its territory widens, and let the bedside one keep the morning scratch.

Does my kitten need both a horizontal scratcher and a vertical post?

Start with both, small. An adult cat's damage tells you its axis, but a kitten has not declared one yet, so offer a horizontal cardboard surface and a vertical post and watch the first weeks: which one the kitten uses after waking, which one after play. Let that use, not a guess, decide the bigger buy later — most kittens settle on one dominant axis, and the other surface stays the spare.

What should I do when my kitten scratches the sofa?

Redirect, never punish. Calmly carry the kitten to the scratcher mid-scratch, set it down at the right surface, and reward the use the moment its paws work there. Then make the wrong surface boring — a temporary throw or cover over the targeted corner — while the right surface stands right beside it. Scolding or spraying teaches the kitten to scratch when you are absent, not to scratch somewhere else.

How often should I trim a kitten's claws?

Little and often. Kitten claws are needle-sharp and hook into fabric easily, so trim tiny amounts — just the sharp tip — in short, calm moments, even if that means one or two claws at a time while the kitten is sleepy. Stop before any protest. Trimming does not replace a scratcher: scratching sheds the sheath, trimming only blunts the tip while the address is being learned.
