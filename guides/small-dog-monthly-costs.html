Costs · Dogs — budgets

A small dog costs more than the food bag. Here is the whole monthly picture, line by line.

Most dog budgets count food and stop. This page lays out every recurring line for one small dog in a small apartment, using published figures only, each one named to its source: the everyday supplies, the prevention and veterinary care that bill yearly instead of monthly, the insurance-or-savings decision, and the walker, rent, and boarding lines that only apartment life creates. Small helps — a small dog eats less and doses smaller — but small does not make the other lines disappear.

Short version

At a glance

  • The everyday lines CareCredit’s dog-cost breakdown puts food at $20 to $200 a month across all dog sizes — a small dog sits toward the low end — with treats at $5 to $22, waste bags at $6 to $19, and at-home grooming supplies at $9 to $49 on top.
  • The invisible lines Synchrony’s 2025 study puts what dog owners actually spend at $1,591 to $2,770 a year. The gap between that and food-only math is prevention, dental care, and the irregular lines below.
  • The decision Insure or self-insure, but choose one: NAPHIA’s 2025 average premium for dog accident-and-illness coverage was $836 a year, and the alternative is a savings buffer you actually keep.

Every figure on this page comes from a published source listed at the bottom, and each range is that source’s range — we have not blended sources into a single invented total. CareCredit’s food range covers every dog size, so read the top of it as large-dog territory; the honest small-dog move is to start at the low end and replace it with your own receipts, the way the closing section describes. These are the costs that show up in an ordinary month. CareCredit’s breakdown of dog ownership costs publishes them as general monthly expenses:

Two honest notes on that table. First, there is no published small-dog-only snapshot to quote, and we will not manufacture one by shrinking a general range — the ranges above are the published dog ranges, with size named as the main lever. Second, a small dog’s real advantage is concentrated in food and in weight-dosed prevention and medicine; rent, boarding per night, an exam fee, and a dental cleaning do not shrink just because the dog does. Budgets that assume “small dog, half of everything” are the ones that fail.

Vet care and prevention do not bill monthly

Dogs carry a prevention stack cats in apartments mostly do not: flea and tick prevention and heartworm prevention run all year. The same CareCredit breakdown puts flea and tick prevention at $29 to $66 and heartworm prevention at $18 to $42 per purchase, a routine vet checkup at $56 to $129 per visit, and dental checkups and cleanings at $300 to $687. The AVMA’s pet-ownership reporting puts the average cost of a veterinary visit for a dog at $214 in 2024 — well above the routine-checkup range, because the average year mixes routine and problem visits.

The budgeting move is boring and it works: total the year you expect — checkups, vaccines as your veterinarian schedules them, a full year of both preventions, a dental reserve — divide by twelve, and let that amount leave your checking account monthly into a separate pet line, whether or not the dog saw a vet that month. A healthy young dog may undershoot the reserve for years. Senior dogs reverse that, which is why our senior small-dog guide treats the floor-level setup and the vet relationship as the same project.

Insurance or a savings buffer

Pet insurance is a risk decision, not a product recommendation, and this page makes none. The published benchmarks: NAPHIA reports that accident-and-illness coverage for a dog averaged $836 a year in the US in 2025 — about $70 a month — while accident-only coverage averaged $190 a year. CareCredit’s breakdown publishes wider monthly premium ranges of $48 to $111 for accident-and-illness and $18 to $42 for accident-only, which is what individual quotes varying by age, breed, and ZIP code look like next to an industry average. Dog coverage costs nearly double the cat average ($435 a year) — dog claims, especially orthopedic ones, run larger.

What the premium is measured against, from the same CareCredit breakdown: an emergency exam runs $107 to $246 before treatment, emergency hospitalization $687 to $1,569, wound treatment $679 to $1,552, and hip dysplasia surgery $2,210 to $5,078. A small dog is not exempt from the list that actually bites apartment dogs — a fall from a sofa, a swallowed object, a dental emergency. So the honest framing is not “insurance, yes or no” but “which mechanism carries a $2,000 surprise: a policy whose exclusions you have read, or a buffer whose balance you know?” Either answer can be right. Having neither is the only wrong one — and pre-existing conditions are generally excluded from new policies, so the decision is worth more made while the dog is young and healthy than after the first diagnosis.

The apartment lines budgets miss

Renters and workers carry lines that general dog-cost guides bury. CareCredit’s breakdown names the lease lines plainly: pet rent usually adds $25 to $75 a month, and a pet deposit or fee can run as much as $500, typically once unless you move. Read yours before the dog arrives, not after; our new small-dog starter kit sequences the gear, and the deposit belongs in that same first-month math.

The workday line is the dog-specific one. A cat uses a litter box at noon; a dog needs a person. CareCredit puts hiring a dog walker at about $300 a month on average — a line that can exceed food, prevention, and insurance combined for a small dog, and the first line to plan honestly if nobody is home midday. Travel compounds it: basic boarding runs $33 to $76 a night, a mid-range kennel $37 to $85, boarding at the vet $48 to $110, and a luxury pet hotel $81 to $185 in the same breakdown — a week away can cost more than a month of everything else. A trusted sitter, a swap with another dog owner, or taking the dog along each change that line differently; our weekend-away kit covers the gear side, and the care arrangement deserves the same planning as the packing.

The lifetime check

Zoom out once, because the monthly view flatters the total. Synchrony’s 2025 Pet Lifetime of Care Study — nearly 5,000 US dog and cat owners surveyed — estimates caring for a dog at $22,125 to $60,602 over a 15-year life, and found nearly 8 in 10 pet owners underestimate what care over a lifetime costs; dog owners in the study guessed $8,158 on average. Nobody needs to hold $60,602 in their head while buying waste bags. But the gap between “about $8,000” and the study’s floor of about $22,000 is the whole argument of this page: the money is mostly not in the monthly lines you see, it is in the yearly and once-a-decade lines you do not — until you budget for them on purpose.

Build your own number

Four steps, using the lines above. One: pick your actual food, treats, and bag lines from your last two months of receipts, not from a range. Two: ask your veterinarian what last year’s routine care and prevention cost and what this year’s should — that conversation is the most accurate figure on this page’s topic you will ever get. Three: choose insurance or buffer, in writing, with a number attached — and price the walker line honestly if your workday needs one. Four: add your lease’s pet rent and your real travel pattern. The result will not match any published average, and that is the point — published averages exist to show you which lines exist, not what your dog costs.

One boundary, stated the way our gear guides state theirs: a dog who stops eating, strains, limps and will not bear weight, or swallows something that is not food is not a budgeting problem to schedule for next month. That dog needs a veterinarian now, and the buffer exists precisely so that decision is never made by a bank balance.

FAQ

How much should I budget per month for one small dog?

Start with the everyday lines — CareCredit puts dog food at $20 to $200 a month across all sizes, with treats, waste bags, and at-home grooming supplies on top — then add the lines that do not bill monthly: routine and dental vet care plus flea, tick, and heartworm prevention spread across the year, either an insurance premium or a savings buffer, any dog walker you actually use, and any pet rent your lease charges. A budget that counts only food is counting a fraction of the dog.

Does a small dog need pet insurance?

No rule requires it. NAPHIA’s 2025 average for dog accident-and-illness coverage was $836 a year — about $70 a month — nearly double the cat average, because dog claims run larger. The alternative is a dedicated savings buffer you actually maintain. What does not work is neither: an emergency exam alone runs $107 to $246 in CareCredit’s breakdown, before any treatment.

Why do dog budgets always come in low?

Because the irregular lines are invisible in a normal month. Synchrony’s 2025 Pet Lifetime of Care Study puts caring for a dog at $22,125 to $60,602 over 15 years, while dog owners in the study guessed $8,158 on average. Dental work, prevention, a walker in a busy month, a year of senior care — none of these appear in the food math, and all of them arrive eventually.
