Order beats a shopping list

Most starter lists are shopping lists: everything a cat might ever use, bought in one anxious weekend. In a small apartment that backfires twice — the floor fills with gear the cat has not chosen yet, and the one station the cat needs in its first hour is still in its box. This guide is the same six picks our other guides name, sequenced by when a new cat actually needs them. Our feeding-station guide builds one pet’s kitchen corner in depth and our litter guide covers the pan-and-mat setup; this page is the whole-apartment order those pieces slot into.

One continuity rule before any gear: where you can, keep the food and litter the cat already knows for the first days. Familiar smells are anchors in an unfamiliar room, and changing everything at once — home, food, litter, routine — gives a stressed cat four reasons to refuse one of them. Change one thing at a time later, gradually.

First: the litter corner

Set this up before the cat comes home. A corner pan spends dead space instead of floor, the hood contains scatter and gives a nervous new cat privacy, and propping the flap open at first — the honest limit on the card — lets the cat learn the door without a barrier lesson on day one. Put it as far from the food station as the apartment allows; in a studio, opposite corners still count.

A scratcher before the sofa teaches it

A cat scratches in its first days in a new room — it is how the room becomes theirs. The only question is what is standing where the scratching happens. The lounge goes parallel to the sofa, in the spot the cat would choose anyway, before the sofa arm becomes the established target; our scratcher guide compares this lounge against the tall post if your cat turns out to be a vertical scratcher. Placement on day one is prevention; the same product bought in week three is a negotiation.

A bed, on offer not on command

Put the bed in a quiet corner, not the middle of the room, and then ignore whether the cat uses it. A new cat sleeps where it feels unobserved — often not the bed — and that is information, not failure: move the bed toward where the cat actually curls, once, and leave it. The Medium is named for its removable washable cover, which matters in week one more than softness does: new-home accidents and stress shedding both land on the bed first.

Solo play comes last

Interactive play in the first days is you, a wand, and short sessions — that is how a new cat learns your hands are safe. The solo station earns its place at the end of the week, when the cat is exploring on its own between your sessions: a heavy wooden box against a wall that it can hunt from without you, and that later doubles as a treat puzzle when the novelty fades, per the card. What to skip the first week

Everything not on this page. A corner groomer solves shedding you have not seen yet — our groomers guide is there when the hair arrives. Steps and ramps solve a height a cat, unlike a small dog, usually does not need help with. A second bed, a second scratcher shape, and toys beyond the box are purchases made before you know this cat’s habits; a week of watching tells you which one it actually needs. One boundary: a cat that is not eating, not using the pan, or hiding without let-up past the first days is not a gear problem — that cat needs a veterinarian, not a seventh product.

FAQ

What should I set up first for a new cat in an apartment?

The litter corner, before the cat arrives if you can: it is the one station a new cat needs in the first hour. Food and water come next, in a quiet spot away from the litter. The scratcher, bed, and play gear can be placed the same day, but the cat will adopt them on its own schedule over the first week.

How much gear does a new apartment cat actually need on day one?

Six stations cover it: a litter pan, a feeder, a water source, a scratcher placed where the cat will want to scratch, a bed it can ignore until it chooses it, and one solo play station. Everything else — groomers, steps, travel gear — solves a problem you have not met yet, and is better bought once you have seen this cat’s habits.

Should I change a new cat’s food and litter right away?

Keep what the cat already knows for the first days where you can — the shelter or previous home’s food and litter are familiar anchors in an unfamiliar room. Change one thing at a time later, gradually, so if the cat goes off something you know which change caused it.
