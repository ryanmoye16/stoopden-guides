Travel · Moving

Moving to a new apartment with a small dog: run the move on the dog’s routine — the familiar bed goes down first, the walk station is ready by the new door, and the first outings run at the old times.

Your dog is not new to you; only the address is new to the dog. A dog settles not by exploring a whole new home at once, but when its day still works: the same bed, the same outings at the same times, and the same kit waiting by the door.

The honest answer: the dog knows the routine, not the address

This guide is the dog counterpart of our moving guide for cats, and the shared rule comes first: relocate, do not replace. It is also the reverse of our new-small-dog starter kit, which is for a dog new to you. A move is different: the dog already knows its bed, its outings and its door routine — what it does not know is the building those things now happen in.

For a dog, the routine is the anchor a cat’s stations are. The daily walking routine — relief first, a midday break, the evening walk as the main event, a last outing before bed — is the part of the old home you can carry intact into the new one. The streets change; the shape of the day does not have to. Everything here follows: one finished corner to land in, the walk station rebuilt by the new door, and first outings smaller than the routine they grow back into.

None of that requires a purchase. This guide names only the three stations the move turns on, in the form the site already names them: if yours are the same models, keep them and skip the links. If one will not survive the move or fit the new room, the picks below are its like-for-like replacements.

Check one: one finished starter corner first

A cat starts a move in one room; a dog starts it in one corner, because a dog’s first need is not territory, it is a place to be put down. Choose the starter corner before moving day — the quiet corner of the room you will sleep in, out of the door swing and away from the entry’s busy zone, in the spirit of our dog bed placement guide. Finish it completely before the dog arrives, even if the rest is still boxes.

The bed is the first thing laid down in it, and it travels unwashed. Its familiar smell is the strongest signal in the new room that this corner is the dog’s — the same principle our overnight stay guide applies to one night, held for as long as settling takes. Washing belongs later; our washing pet beds guide covers that wash, including the rule that the foam itself never soaks. Around it: water apart from the bed, food where it will stay, nothing still undecided.

Check two: the walk station, before the first walk

At the old home, outings ran from a station: leash in its spot, dispenser clipped to it, bottle filled — the setup our dog-walk station guide builds by the door. In the new home it is set up by the new door before the first outing, not found in a box after it. The first walk from a new home is a large event for a dog; it should at least start from the old kit, grabbed the old way, with no searching while the dog waits at a strange door.

The leash, collar and harness stay criteria-only here — no brand is named for them, because fit and habit matter more than any label. Use the leash the dog already walks on, and check that whatever it wears still fits as it did at the old home. A new harness bought for the move would be one more unfamiliar thing on the first outing.

The pair below is the whole station’s named half, and nothing joins it for the move: no second dispenser for a spare leash, no new bottle in a moving-day style. The dispenser earns its place on every trip, because a dog does not save its relief for familiar ground. The bottle earns its place on the longer first outings, served in its cup while standing still, as our road trip guide serves water at stops.

Check three: first outings, on the old clock

The first outings from the new home are relief trips, not tours. Keep them short: out of the new door, around the new block on one route, relief, and home — the pattern our puppy first walks guide uses, borrowed for a grown dog in a new place. One route, repeated, teaches the dog where relief happens here and how the trip home works.

Run those trips at the old times. Relief first in the morning, the midday break, the evening outing, the last trip before bed — the routine’s hours survive the move even when its distances shrink. A dog whose outings arrive when they always have has a clock to hang the new streets on, and the evening walk can grow back into the main event within days.

Expect house-training regression, and read it correctly. A dog that was reliable for years in the old home may have accidents in the new one, because the lesson it learned was tied to the old doors, route and ground. That is relearning, not defiance. Answer it the puppy way: more frequent short trips, thorough cleaning of any accident spot so it does not become a rival indoor address, and no scolding. The accidents should thin as the route and the corner become familiar.

Check four: hazards first, doors shut on moving day

Before the dog arrives, walk the new place once at the dog’s height and do the hazard sweep. Look for what a small dog can reach, chew, slip through or knock over in a home not yet arranged: loose cords, cleaning chemicals stored low or still out from the pre-move clean, open windows without secure screens, escape gaps at doors and stairwells, and small moving debris — screws, clips, packing offcuts — on the floors. Fix or shut away what you find before the dog is in the building, not after its first explore.

On the day itself, the dog is never loose around the move. While furniture is moving and doors stand open, the dog is shut in a finished room — the room holding the starter corner, door closed, water down — or it is out of the building entirely with a sitter. It is not weaving between movers, and not watched by whoever happens to be free, which in practice is nobody. Open doors plus strangers plus a loose dog is how a move acquires an escape.

Check five: the paperwork that moves too

A move changes the dog’s records as well as its rooms, and this part is a checklist, not a purchase. Work through it in the first days: the address and contact details on the dog’s ID tag, the details held against its microchip, and the vet records — whether the current practice stays the practice at the new address, or the records need to travel to a new one. Keep the documents with the dog’s stations, not in a general moving box.

The test is simple: every place that would be used to get this dog home, or to treat it, should point at the new address and a number that is answered — each item checked off, not assumed.

The moving-day sequence

Sweep and finish first. Hazard sweep done, starter corner finished, bed down unwashed, walk station by the new door — all before the dog travels.

The dog is shut away, or out, while furniture moves. A finished room with the door closed, or a sitter away from the building. Never loose around open doors and movers.

Unpack the dog’s stations before your own. Bed corner, food and water, and the walk station are completed before your kitchen or clothes. The dog’s day should work on the first evening, even if yours does not.

First outing, then the old clock. A short relief trip on the one route on arrival, then meals and outings at the old times. The paperwork checklist follows in the first days.

Buy or skip

A move is another guide here where the default purchase is nothing: the gear that settles a dog is the gear already standing in the old apartment. The panels below are for the exceptions.

Buy only if

  • A station genuinely will not survive the move. A bed torn in transit earns a like-for-like replacement — same job, flat form, same starter corner.
  • A station does not fit the new room. Measure first; if the old station honestly cannot fit, replace that one on fit, not looks.
  • The dog has no walk station yet. If outings still ran on pockets and loose bags, build the kit now, and let the new door be where the habit starts.

Skip if

  • The plan is a whole new gear set for the new place. New gear on a new place is two changes at once; the familiar set settles the dog faster than any matching new one.
  • The old stations are merely worn. Wear is familiarity: the bed slept on for a year settles the dog faster than its pristine replacement. Replace on failure or fit.
  • You are buying to solve first-days behaviour. Restlessness and an accident or two in the first days are a move working normally, not a missing product. Give the routine time.

When it is not a gear problem

A move unsettles dogs, and the first days may look like it: exploring at odd hours, sleeping less deeply, checking unfamiliar doors. That arc should bend, though — a little more of the old dog each day, eating on schedule, outings taken willingly, accidents thinning. When it does not bend, a dog that is not eating or drinking, refuses outings it took willingly before, or stays distressed without let-up past the first days needs a veterinarian, not more gear.

FAQ

Should I buy a whole new gear set for the new apartment?

No. A new home is one large change, and a whole new gear set is a second change landing in the same week. Move the stations the dog already uses, exactly as they are, and buy only a like-for-like replacement if one will not survive the move or does not fit the new room. Measure first, so any replacement is chosen on fit, not moving-week panic.

Should I wash my dog’s bed before the move?

No. Travel it unwashed. On moving day the familiar smell on the flat bed is the point — it is the part of the old home the dog can still find in the new one, laid down first in the starter corner. Washing belongs after the dog has settled; before the move, leave the bed exactly as the dog last slept on it.

What should the first walks from the new home be like?

Short relief trips, on one route, at the old times. Set the walk station up by the new door before the first outing, then go out and back around the new block on the familiar leash kit. The route grows later, once the dog reads the new streets as its own; the first days are for relief and return, not exploring.

What if my dog has accidents in the new apartment?

Treat it as normal, temporary regression, not defiance. A dog that was reliable in the old home is relearning where relief happens in a new one. Go back to the puppy pattern: more frequent short trips at the old times, thorough cleaning of any accident spot, and patience. The accidents should thin as the routine settles.

What if my dog will not settle, eat, or go out after the move?

Give the first days some patience: a finished starter corner, the old feeding and outing times, and a quiet home. But a dog that is not eating or drinking, refuses outings it took willingly before, or stays distressed without let-up past the first days is not a gear problem. That dog needs a veterinarian, not a purchase.
