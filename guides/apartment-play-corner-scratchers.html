Play · Play corner

A play corner is for the cat that scratches both ways.

Two named picks, one strip of floor: a cardboard lounge takes the horizontal rake along the sofa, a tall sisal post at its end takes the standing pull — both between the cat and the real fabric.

How the corner works

Our scratcher sofa vs posts guide is a choosing guide: watch the stolen corner of the real sofa, match the dominant motion, and buy one sacrificial object. This guide starts where that one ends — with the cats it names who do both. If your sofa shows a forward rake along the cushion and a stand-and-pull up the arm, a single object covers only half the damage, and the uncovered half keeps landing on the upholstery.

The corner is an L, not a collection. The lounge runs parallel to the sofa, where the horizontal rake already happens and where a nap spot is allowed to be. The post stands at the sofa’s end, near the arm that takes the vertical pull, on a base that does not skate. Both objects stay on the cat’s existing route — near sleep, near the doorway they patrol — because a play corner in a room the cat only passes through is storage, not a station.

Sequence matters more than the pair. Start with the dominant axis, exactly as the choosing guide says, and let it pick up scent and use. For the horizontal scratch: the lounge

A reclined cardboard lounge works because it is the right height for a lying or sitting rake, and because it doubles as a perch and a bed — which is what earns a wide object its floor in a small living room. It sheds corrugate crumbs and the cardboard collapses over time; plan to vacuum and to flip, then replace, the core.

For the vertical scratch: the post

When the corner is wrong

Skip the second object for a one-axis cat — a post bought for a purely horizontal shredder is a tree-shaped suggestion, and a lounge bought for a purely vertical puller is a bed with delusions. Skip the corner entirely if the strip along the sofa is your only walkway, or if the only available corner is a hallway the cat crosses but never settles in; one object placed on the actual crime scene beats two objects placed hopefully. And if the cat still prefers the upholstery with both objects in place, the problem is location or axis, not quantity — move the objects onto the stolen spot before buying anything else.

FAQ

Does a cat need both a scratcher lounge and a scratching post?

Only a cat that scratches both ways. If the damage on the real sofa is one axis — a forward rake along a cushion, or a stand-and-pull up the arm — one object in that axis is the whole answer, and our scratcher guide says to start there. A corner with both earns its floor when both motions already happen: the lounge takes the horizontal rake and doubles as a nap spot, the post takes the full standing stretch.

Where should an apartment play corner go?

On the cat’s existing route, not in an unused pet corner. Cats scratch near where they sleep and near the doorway they patrol, and they scratch the real sofa because it is already there. Put the corner against or beside the real sofa — lounge parallel to it, post at its end — so both objects sit between the cat and the fabric. Keep the lounge out of the kitchen-to-door traffic lane.

How much floor does a play corner take?

Less than it looks, because the two shapes stack along one wall instead of spreading. The lounge is low and wide — about 34 inches long — and runs parallel to the sofa like a piece of furniture. The post is tall and narrow on a 16 by 16 inch base, so it takes vertical room, not floor. The real cost is the strip along the sofa: if that strip is your only walkway, one object is the honest setup.
