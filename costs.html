How to budget in a small apartment

Most pet budgets fail the same way gear fails in a small home: they are built for a different life. A budget that counts only food and litter is counting roughly half the pet, and the half it misses is the half that arrives as a surprise. The short version of this section is below; the first guide works through every line for one indoor cat.

Count every line, not just the visible ones

CareCredit’s published snapshot puts a cat’s everyday lines — food and treats, litter, litter-box maintenance, enrichment toys, and grooming — at $75 to $100 or more a month. That snapshot is the floor for a normal month, not the ceiling for a bad one, and it deliberately excludes veterinary care, insurance, and rent. The indoor cat monthly costs guide lays out each line with its range and what moves it, so you can replace the ranges with your own receipts.

Vet care bills yearly, so save for it monthly

A routine checkup runs $57 to $130 per visit and dental care $59 to $719 in the same CareCredit breakdown, and the AVMA puts the average cost of a veterinary visit for a cat at $138 in 2024. None of that bills monthly, so a monthly budget has to carry it as a monthly set-aside: total the year you expect, divide by twelve, and let that amount leave checking whether or not the pet saw a vet. Senior pets reverse the pattern — the senior-cat guide treats the floor-level setup and the vet relationship as the same project.

Insure or save, but choose one

Insurance is a risk decision, and this site recommends no plan. The benchmarks are published ones: NAPHIA reports cat accident-and-illness coverage averaged $435 a year in the US in 2025, while an emergency room base fee alone averaged $113 to $260 before treatment in Synchrony’s 2025 procedural cost study. The honest question is which mechanism carries a $2,000 surprise — a policy whose exclusions you have read, or a buffer whose balance you know. Having neither is the only wrong answer, and the choice is worth more made while the pet is young, because pre-existing conditions are generally excluded from new policies.

The renter lines come first

Pet rent can add up to $50 a month to a lease and a pet deposit can run as much as $500, and boarding while you travel runs $22 to $108 a night — all from the same CareCredit breakdown. Read the lease before the pet arrives; the deposit belongs in the same first-month math as the new-cat starter kit and the small-dog starter kit, and the care arrangement for a trip deserves the same planning as the weekend-away kit.

Then replace the ranges with your own number

Published averages exist to show you which lines exist, not what your pet costs. The working method, from the cat guide: take your food and litter lines from your last two months of receipts, ask your veterinarian what last year’s routine care cost and what this year’s should, choose insurance or buffer in writing with a number attached, and add your lease’s pet rent and your real travel pattern. The result will not match any published average, and that is the point — it will match your apartment, your lease, and your pet, which is the only budget that survives contact with a real year.

Figures on this page are published US ranges and averages, not quotes; your veterinarian’s fees and your lease govern your actual costs. This section is budgeting information, not financial or veterinary advice.
